Is my 2026 assessment fair?

Most Mineral Point assessments jumped this year because the City re-valued every home to today's prices. A big increase isn't automatically unfair. What matters is whether your home is assessed at the same share of its value as everyone else's. This page helps you check in about five minutes.

Made by a Mineral Point resident to help neighbors check their own assessments. It is not from the City of Mineral Point or Equity Appraisal, and it is not an appraisal or legal advice.

It can't see condition: a dated home and a renovated one look alike to this math. Treat a high ratio as a reason to look closer, not proof.

The one idea behind it

Wisconsin law says every home must be assessed at the same percentage of what it would sell for. So the test is a simple division:

Your assessed value÷Your home's market value=Your ratio

Source: Wisconsin Department of Revenue, 2026 Guide for Property Owners (PB-060), Section III, "Equitable Assessment," page 10.

Recent Mineral Point sales are typically assessed at about 99% of their value (the middle of 18 recent City sales; see "Where the numbers come from" at the bottom of this page). If your ratio is close to that, your assessment is in line with your neighbors'. If it's well above, you may be paying more than your share.

Bringing older prices to January 1, 2026

Assessments value every home as of January 1, 2026, so a price from an earlier date has to be brought forward to that date (and a 2026 price brought back).

Each year's figure is the Wisconsin Department of Revenue's estimate of how much City of Mineral Point home values changed because of the market (not new construction) during that calendar year. A price is grown by each year's rate for the part of that year it covers. The state won't publish 2026 until August 2027, so 2026 prices are brought back at an estimated 7% (the hollow point), the state's latest figure. Statewide, the median sale price was up about 6% in the first half of 2026 (Wisconsin REALTORS Association), which supports that estimate. Years before 2015 use the 2015–2025 average of about 7%. These figures measure the market; they are not a limit on assessments. Source: Statements of Changes in Equalized Values, Iowa County, 2016–2026 (older reports via the Internet Archive).

%

One rate is applied to every year (the dashed line on the chart). The City's yearly rates averaged about 7% from 2015 to 2025. A typical year was closer to 5%, but 2021 and 2022 were about 19%. One rate will under- or over-state growth depending on when the price was set, so the state's yearly rates are usually more accurate.

    If your ratio is high: what to do next

    Most people never get past step 1. Calling is free, and errors on the property record (wrong square footage, bathrooms, basement) are often fixed on the spot.

    1. Start here

      Call the assessor

      Equity Appraisal, LLC does the City's assessments. Useful things to ask for:

      • Your property record card. It lists what the City has on file: square footage, bedrooms, bathrooms, basement, garage, condition. Check every line for errors.
      • The sales they used to value your home, with the date and price of each.
      • How your value was calculated: the land value, the building value, and any adjustment applied to your neighborhood.

      These are public records. If you can't get them from the assessor, the City Clerk can provide them.

      equityappraisalsm@yahoo.com · 608-826-0009

    2. At least 48 hours before the Board meets

      Tell the City Clerk you plan to object

      If talking doesn't resolve it, you can appeal to the Board of Review. For 2026 it meets Wednesday, October 14. You must give the clerk notice first.

      cityclerk@cityofmineralpointwi.gov · 608-987-2361

    3. The clerk asked for it by October 12

      Fill out the objection form

      Form PA-115A asks what you think your home is worth and why. Send the completed form to the City Clerk.

      Form PA-115A (PDF)

      Bring your evidence

      Bring copies of whatever supports your value, such as sales of similar homes or your purchase records. The state's guide explains how the hearing works.

    Where the numbers come from

    Two checks sit behind this page.

    How the City assessed homes that recently sold (where the 99% comes from)

    The idea. A sale is the best evidence of what a home is worth. So for homes that sold recently, the City's 2026 assessment can be compared with the price a buyer actually paid. If the City got every home exactly right, each assessment would be 100% of its price.

    How it was done. The check uses 18 ordinary sales (between unrelated buyers and sellers, not foreclosures or family transfers) from 2024–2026. Because assessments value homes as of January 1, 2026, each price is first adjusted to that date using the method chosen in step 2. Then each assessment is divided by that adjusted price.

    What it shows. The ratios spread out, which is normal: the assessor values homes from property records and a pricing model, not one sale at a time, and a record can't see condition, updates or an unusual sale. The middle ratio is 99%. That's the yardstick your own result is compared with: if your ratio is close to it, you're assessed like your neighbors.

    How to read the table

    Sold for is the actual price. Price on Jan 1, 2026 is that price adjusted to the assessment date. Assessed at is the City's 2026 value. Ratio is the assessment divided by the adjusted price; colors match the result scale in step 3. Tap a column heading to sort.

    Do the state's yearly rates match what Mineral Point homes actually did?

    The idea. Step 2 grows older prices using the state's yearly rates. A good way to test those rates is with homes that sold twice: grow the first price by the rates and see how close it lands to the second price.

    How it was done. The check uses 12 City homes that sold twice, held about 2 years or more. Quick resales and fix-and-flip sales are left out, because their price jumps come from renovations, not the market.

    What it shows. In other words, the state's rates are a reasonable guide but may run a little high for individual older homes. Each home differs in condition and upkeep, so this is a rough check, not a correction.

    How to read the table

    State's rates predict is the first price grown to the second sale date. Actual vs. predicted shows how far the real second price landed from that prediction; a perfect match would be 0%.
